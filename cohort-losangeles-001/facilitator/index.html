Facilitation principles

Before you open the room

The same four habits carry every session. Everything below is detail on top of these.

Timer visible, always

Share your screen with a running timer during every timed block. Silence is more persuasive than interrupting.

Vague → specific

“Grow sales” gets one follow-up: “what number, by when?” Don't let it pass uncaptured.

Call on the quiet ones

Airtime skews to whoever talks first. Name the person who hasn't spoken before moving on.

End every block with a stated action

No block closes on discussion alone. Someone says out loud what happens next, and you write it down.

1

Equal airtime beats having smart people in the room

The best-known study on group intelligence found that a group's collective performance was not predicted by the average or maximum IQ of its members. What predicted it: how evenly conversational turns were distributed, and how socially perceptive members were. Groups where one or two people dominated performed worse — on every kind of task.

Do this: keep a visible tally of who has spoken. Before closing any block, name the person who hasn't. “Sudeep, you haven't weighed in — what are you seeing?” This is the single highest-leverage thing a facilitator does.
Woolley, Chabris, Pentland, Hashmi & Malone, “Evidence for a Collective Intelligence Factor in the Performance of Human Groups,” Science, 2010
AlsoTony RobbinsRobbins’ frame is that where focus goes, energy flows — and in a group the facilitator is the one directing focus. If you let attention drift to whoever is loudest, that’s a choice you made by not making one.Robbins — focus & stateSeth GodinGodin’s “people like us do things like this” is about culture as observed behaviour. Whatever you tolerate in session one becomes the norm — if one person runs the room and nobody stops it, that is the culture now.Godin, This Is Marketing
2

People only say the useful thing if it's safe to be wrong

Psychological safety — the shared belief that you won't be punished for speaking up — is what separates teams that learn from teams that don't. When Google studied 180 of its own teams, it was the strongest differentiator they found, ahead of who was on the team.

In a peer room this is the whole ballgame. Members arrived to talk about what isn't working. If the first person to admit a real problem gets advice-splained, nobody else will go there.

Do this: go first with your own unresolved problem, and be specific about it. Frame the session as a learning problem, not a performance review. When someone shares something hard, thank them out loud before anyone responds.
Edmondson, “Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams,” ASQ, 1999 · Google Project Aristotle
AlsoTony RobbinsRobbins works state before strategy: a person who feels judged is physiologically defensive, and no amount of good advice reaches them there. Change how the room feels first, then work the problem.Robbins — stateAlex HormoziHormozi is blunt that the useful conversation is the one about what’s actually broken. A room where everyone reports that things are going well is a room producing nothing.Hormozi — constraint thinking
3

Everyone writes before anyone talks

Open group brainstorming reliably produces fewer and lower-quality ideas than the same people thinking alone and pooling afterward. Two reasons: you can't form a thought while listening to someone else (production blocking), and once an idea is in the air the group anchors on it.

Do this: before any open discussion — hot seats especially — give 60–90 seconds of silence for everyone to write their answer down. Then go around. You'll get ideas that would never have surfaced, from people who'd otherwise have stayed quiet.
Diehl & Stroebe, “Productivity Loss in Brainstorming Groups,” JPSP, 1987 · overview
AlsoJames ClearClear’s third law is make it easy — lower the friction on the behaviour you want. Speaking up cold in front of five peers is high-friction; reading something you already wrote down is not.Clear, Atomic Habits
4

Groups default to discussing what everyone already knows

Deliberating groups fail in four predictable ways: they amplify individual errors rather than cancel them, they follow informational cascades (people echo whoever spoke first), they polarize toward more extreme versions of their starting view, and they suffer hidden profiles — the group talks about shared information and never surfaces what only one person knows.

That last one is why a room of six can still miss the obvious answer: the person holding it assumed it was already known, or didn't think it was their place.

Do this: ask for unshared information directly. “Who here has actually done this before?” and “What does everyone think we're missing?” And speak last — if the facilitator opinionates first, you've set the anchor for the whole room.
Sunstein & Hastie, “Four Failures of Deliberating Groups” · Wiser, HBR Press, 2015
AlsoSeth GodinGodin’s Purple Cow argument: the safe, agreeable answer is the one everyone already has, and it is therefore worth nothing. The useful contribution is the one that sounds slightly wrong at first.Godin, Purple CowAlex HormoziHormozi’s version of hidden profiles: the real constraint is usually the thing the founder hasn’t said out loud yet — sometimes because they haven’t admitted it to themselves.Hormozi — constraint thinking
5

The agenda should be questions, not topics

Meeting research is blunt about agendas: having one doesn't help by itself — plenty of terrible meetings have agendas. What helps is framing each block as a question the room has to answer, because a question has an obvious finish line and a topic doesn't. “Pairings” can run forever. “Who is each person paired with, and what do they owe each other by Friday?” ends when it's answered.

Do this: the run-of-shows below are already written as outcomes. Before each block, say the question out loud. Before you move on, confirm it's been answered.
Rogelberg, The Surprising Science of Meetings, Oxford University Press, 2019
AlsoTony RobbinsA signature Robbins line: the quality of your life is the quality of your questions. Same for a meeting — a block framed as a question has an answer and therefore an ending. A block framed as a topic does not.Robbins — quality questionsDan MartellMartell’s 10-80-10 habit: define the outcome and the standard at the front, let the room do the middle 80%, and close it yourself. Vague at the start guarantees rework at the end.Martell, Buy Back Your Time
6

Nothing closes on discussion alone

The most common way a good conversation produces nothing is that it ends warmly and vaguely. Everyone leaves feeling it went well; no one can say what happens next. The fix is mechanical, not motivational — someone states the action out loud, in their own words, and it gets written down where they'll see it again.

Do this: every block ends with a named person saying a specific next action and a date. You write it down live, on screen, so the room watches it get recorded. That visibility is most of the accountability.
Rogelberg, The Surprising Science of Meetings · consistent with the commitment literature on public, specific goals
AlsoJames ClearClear’s core claim: you do not rise to the level of your goals, you fall to the level of your systems. A stated intention is a goal. A named action, on a date, written where they’ll see it again is the beginning of a system.Clear, Atomic HabitsDan MartellMartell would ask who owns it and when it’s due, every time. An action without a name attached is a wish the whole room quietly agreed to ignore.Martell, Buy Back Your Time

Session scripts

The four run-of-shows

Week 1 · Map
Intros + open forum
Mon Aug 17, 5:30–7:00 PM PT
5:30Hellos — 5 min. Names, where everyone's dialing in from. Set the rule out loud: “no pitching — real business, real constraint.” State the confidentiality norm. Start the Meet timer (Activities → Timer) — everyone sees the same clock all night.
5:35Deck — 5 min. What BizColab is, why it exists, how the four Mondays run. Land on the format for the next block before you stop sharing.
5:40Business + one challenge — 3 min hard each × 6 (ends 5:58). Spec it out loud first: ~60 sec on the business, ~90 sec on the one challenge with today's number, ~30 sec on what help they'd take. Hold all feedback — cut in if the room starts solving. Log each person's challenge + ask as they go.
5:58Break — 5 minutes. Say the return time out loud (6:03), set the Meet timer to 5:00, restart on the clock; cameras off is fine.
6:03Open forum — 8 min per person × 6 (ends 6:51). One person's challenge on the table at a time; no speaking order, the room just works it. Before you close each block, call on someone who hasn't spoken yet — open forum has no built-in airtime protection and two or three voices will otherwise fill all six blocks. Log direction / offer / ask per person — that log is the Week 2 pairing map.
6:51Close — 9 min. Preview Week 2 (pairings + first guest). Name the one thing to do before next Monday. Remind: Slack + member page password.
Watch for

The open forum going quiet, or the same two voices carrying it — name someone directly. One or two people eating the clock in intros — cut in kindly at the 2:30 mark. Quiet members getting skipped in the energy. Challenges that are really symptoms (“need more leads”) — note it, revisit in Week 2 pairing.

Week 2 · Match
Pairings
Mon Aug 24, 5:30–7:00 PM PT
5:30Check-in: 30 sec each — one line on where their challenge stands.
5:35Guest operator: 10 min talk, 10 min Q&A. Moderate questions; cut off at 20 min flat even mid-answer.
5:55Break — 5 minutes. Say the time out loud and restart on the clock; cameras off is fine.
6:00Reveal pairings — build this map before the session from the Session 1 offers/needs doc, don't do it live. Each pair states what they'll deliver for the other by Friday.
6:15Feedback rounds, 3 presenters × ~11 min (3 present, then room feedback). Ask the room directly: “one thing you'd change.”
6:50Wrap: confirm pair deliverables, confirm who presents in Week 3's hot seats.
Watch for

Guest running long — agree a hard stop with them beforehand. Presenters being too polished; ask for the actual number, not the narrative. Uneven feedback — make sure everyone gives at least one comment across the three rounds.

Week 3 · Move
Hot seats
Mon Aug 31, 5:30–7:00 PM PT
5:30Check-in round.
5:35Optional guest #2 (8 talk + 7 Q&A, ends 5:50) — if skipped, start hot seats early and add a 4th.
5:50Hot seats 1 & 2 — 20 min each. 5 min: presenter frames the problem — interrupt if it drifts, ask “what's the one decision you need help with?” 15 min: room solves. Your job is to keep it generating options, not sympathy — call on whoever hasn't spoken.
6:30Break — 5 minutes. Say the time out loud and restart on the clock; cameras off is fine.
6:35Hot seat 3 — 20 min. Same shape: 5 min framing, 15 min the room solves.
6:55Each hot-seat presenter states their next action out loud.
Watch for

Pick the most stuck people for hot seats, not the loudest volunteers — check ship reports beforehand. Advice turning into “when I…” monologues — redirect to a specific, actionable ask.

Week 4 · Measure
Results
Mon Sep 14, 5:30–7:00 PM PT — moved off Sep 7 for Labor Day
5:30Open.
5:35Results round, 5–6 min each (ends 6:20). Prompt: commitment, what shipped, the number then vs. now, what's next. Hold people to the actual number.
6:20Break — 5 minutes. Say the time out loud and restart on the clock; cameras off is fine.
6:25Honest group post-mortem on what didn't move and why.
6:40Set standing accountability pairs and a recurring cadence. Gauge interest in Cohort 02 referrals.
6:55Close — group photo / screenshot.
Watch for

Letting the narrative replace the number — ask directly for the metric from Week 1 vs. today. Make sure every pair actually exchanges contact info before the call ends.

Troubleshooting

When the room isn't working

Most cohort problems show up as one of these. None of them fix themselves, and all of them get worse if you wait a week.

What you're seeingWhat to do about it, that session
One person is running the room Go to a strict round-robin for the rest of the block — no open floor. Privately, after: thank them for engaging and ask them to hold back until three others have spoken. Almost always they don't realise.
Seth GodinGodin’s point about norms cuts both ways — the dominant person is usually reading the room correctly and filling a silence nobody else claimed. Change the format, not the person.Godin — culture as behaviour
Someone has gone quiet for two sessions Reach out between Mondays, not during. Quiet usually means their challenge got stale or they're embarrassed nothing moved. Give them a slot to reset it publicly — that's a normal outcome, not a failure.
James ClearClear’s rule is never miss twice. One bad week is noise; two starts becoming an identity. Intervene between sessions, while it’s still one.Clear, Atomic HabitsTony RobbinsRobbins would treat this as state, not laziness — people withdraw from what makes them feel incompetent. Give them a win they can actually get before asking for a bigger commitment.Robbins — state
Advice-giving instead of experience Interrupt and reframe on the spot: “have you done this yourself, or is it a hunch?” Both are allowed, but the room needs to know which it's hearing. Say it once early and it holds for four weeks.

The business behind the room

Why the program is built this way

If you're running a cohort, these are the decisions that determine whether it becomes something durable or stays a nice group of people who met for a month. Adapted from an Alex Hormozi–framework review of the Cohort 01 model — useful mechanics, applied in BizColab's voice.

The equation to keep in your head

Value = (dream outcome × likelihood it happens) ÷ (time it takes × effort required)

Every design choice in this program moves one of those four terms. Four weeks instead of twelve shortens time. Six people instead of thirty raises likelihood, because everyone gets real airtime. Ninety minutes a week plus thirty minutes a day is a deliberately small effort ask.

The weakest term for any new cohort is likelihood — a first cohort has no proof it works. That's the problem the next two cards solve.

AlsoSeth GodinGodin would attack the same term differently — not with proof but with the smallest viable audience. Six people who badly want this beat sixty who might.Godin, This Is Marketing
Non-negotiable

Capture the starting number in Session 1

You cannot show a transformation you never baselined. If a member says “more qualified leads” and you don't write down that they had four last month, then in Week 4 you have a nice story and no evidence.

  • Every commitment gets logged as where it is today → where it's going → by when, with the real figure
  • Log it somewhere durable the same night — not just spoken aloud
  • Week 4 is then a comparison, not a recollection

This costs fifteen minutes and is the difference between a second cohort you have to sell and one that sells itself.

AlsoJames ClearClear’s tracking principle applies to the program too: what gets measured gets managed — and what never got a baseline can’t be either.Clear, Atomic Habits
Timing matters more than you'd think

Collect proof at the moment of the win

The instinct is to ask for testimonials at the end, in the wrap-up. That's the worst moment — people are tired and summarising. Ask right after something breaks open: a hot seat that cracks a problem in Week 3, an intro that lands, a number that moves. Sixty seconds of phone video, right then, beats a polished paragraph a month later.

AlsoTony RobbinsRobbins’ peak state logic — testimony captured at the emotional high is more specific and more credible than the same person’s considered summary a month later.Robbins — peak state
Pricing

A founding cohort is priced once

A first cohort with no track record can and probably should be cheap — you're buying proof, not revenue. Cohort 01 ran at $99 for exactly that reason.

The trap is repeating it. Price is not only revenue here, it's an attendance filter: a room that costs nothing gets treated like it costs nothing, and this format breaks if three people drift. Decide the next cohort's number before the current one ends, and say it out loud to the room — it makes the founding rate feel like what it is and gives members a reason to refer someone now.

Worth adding once you have proof

A guarantee, tied to the process

Guarantee the thing you control, never someone else's business results. Something like: show up to all four, do the weekly work, and if your challenge hasn't measurably moved, the next cohort is on us. It removes the main objection and costs you one seat in the worst case.

AlsoSeth GodinGodin frames a guarantee as a trust signal rather than a discount — it says you’ve seen this work often enough to take the risk yourself.Godin, This Is Marketing
The question to answer honestly

What is this actually for?

Six seats at a founding price is not a business on its own — it's roughly six hours of live facilitation for a few hundred dollars. That's fine, as long as you know which of these you're building:

  • A standalone product — then price, proof and a continuity offer are everything
  • A front door — graduates roll into an ongoing paid room, which is where the economics actually work
  • A relationship channel — the real return is depth with a handful of operators per quarter, in which case optimise purely for room quality and how memorable it is, and stop worrying about the fee

All three are legitimate. Running one while quietly measuring yourself against another is what makes a good program feel like a failure.

Onboarding

Running your own cohort

If you're picking this up to facilitate a BizColab cohort, this is the shortest path from nothing to a room that works.

Before Week 1

  • Read the run-of-shows end to end — not just Week 1. Knowing where Week 4 lands changes how you run Week 1.
  • Cap the room at six. Every member needs an uninterrupted intro plus a full block of the room working their challenge. At seven or eight the ninety minutes stop adding up.
  • Screen for one thing: a real business with a real constraint, and a willingness to say it out loud. Not stage, revenue or industry.
  • Set up the recurring meeting link and calendar invites for all sessions at once, and a group chat for between-session momentum.
  • Prepare your own challenge. You go first, and it has to be genuine. A facilitator who won't be vulnerable gets a room that won't either.

Every session

  • Visible timer, shared on screen. The clock enforces the format so you don't have to.
  • Track airtime. A tally next to each name. Name whoever hasn't spoken before you close a block.
  • Silent writing before open discussion — 60 to 90 seconds, every time.
  • Speak last on anything substantive, so you don't anchor the room.
  • Every block ends with a named person, a specific action, and a date, written down live.
  • Take the five-minute break. Ninety minutes without one costs you the last thirty.

After every session

  • Post the commitments where members will see them again before next week.
  • Fill in the session log below while it's fresh — summary, commitments, decisions, next steps.
  • Note who was quiet and reach out before the next session, not during it.
  • Capture proof when a win happens, in the moment — see the business section above.
The one rule under all of it

You are not the product

The temptation, especially if you know a lot, is to be the most useful person in the room. Resist it. Your job is to make everyone in the room useful to each other — the moment the room is oriented toward you, it stops being a peer group and becomes an audience, and audiences don't hold each other accountable.
